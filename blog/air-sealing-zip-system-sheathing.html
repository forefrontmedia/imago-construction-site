Why Air Sealing and ZIP System Sheathing Matter More Than R-Value Alone

Insulation gets all the marketing attention. Here's why the air barrier underneath it is doing just as much of the work.

B07Feature image · 16:9

Most homeowners shopping for a builder ask about insulation R-value, because it's the number that gets marketed. Far fewer ask about air sealing, even though it's arguably the more important half of the equation. Here's why that distinction matters.

It's a technical topic, but it's worth understanding in plain terms, because it's one of the few building decisions that's genuinely difficult to fix after the fact.

Insulation slows heat transfer. Air sealing stops air movement.

These are two different jobs. Insulation resists heat moving through a solid material (a wall, a ceiling). Air sealing prevents actual air, and the moisture and energy it carries, from leaking through gaps, seams, and penetrations. A home can have excellent insulation and still perform poorly if the air barrier underneath it is full of gaps.

What ZIP System sheathing actually does

We use ZIP System sheathing on our framing, which combines structural sheathing with a built-in weather-resistant barrier, taped at every seam during framing. That creates a continuous air and water barrier across the entire exterior of the home before insulation or siding ever goes on, rather than relying on a separate wrap installed later with more opportunities for gaps.

Because the barrier is built into the sheathing itself and taped seam by seam as framing happens, there's no separate step where a house wrap gets stapled up weeks later, often after wind, rain, or job-site traffic has already worked its way into small gaps. It's a more reliable sequence, not just a different material.

Why this shows up in your utility bill

Uncontrolled air leakage is one of the largest sources of energy loss in a typical home, often larger than the insulation itself. A well-sealed envelope means your HVAC system isn't constantly fighting outside air leaking in and conditioned air leaking out, which is the most direct, measurable way building science translates into a lower monthly bill.

We've seen well-sealed, properly insulated homes run noticeably lower utility bills than similarly sized older homes nearby, even before accounting for equipment efficiency, simply because the building itself isn't fighting outside air all day.

Why it also affects comfort, not just cost

Drafts, cold spots, and rooms that never quite match the thermostat setting are almost always an air sealing problem, not an insulation or HVAC sizing problem. Sealing the envelope properly is what makes every room in the house feel the same temperature, year-round.

Why it matters for durability

Uncontrolled air leakage carries moisture into wall cavities, which is one of the leading causes of hidden rot and mold over time. A properly sealed, taped envelope keeps that moisture where it belongs: outside.

The question worth asking your builder

Ask specifically: "Do you test the home's air tightness with a blower door test, and can I see the result?" A builder who tests and shares that number is building to a real, verifiable standard. A builder who can only describe insulation type is only telling you half the story.

If a builder hesitates or can't give you a specific air-changes-per-hour number, that's worth noting. It usually means air sealing isn't being treated as a measured, verified part of construction, just an assumption.

How this fits into the bigger performance picture

Air sealing doesn't work in isolation. It has to be paired with the right mechanical ventilation, typically an energy recovery ventilator, so the home still gets a steady supply of fresh air even though it's no longer relying on accidental leaks to breathe. A tightly sealed home without deliberate ventilation can actually perform worse for indoor air quality, which is why we treat these as one connected system rather than separate checkboxes.

This is also where a blower door test earns its value: it tells us, with an actual number, how tight the envelope is before we finalize the ventilation strategy, rather than guessing and hoping the two systems balance correctly once you've already moved in.
